I’ve been working on turning my projects into a portfolio that shows what I can actually build, rather than simply listing technologies on a resume.

One of the biggest wins was taking my FlyRank Machine Learning internship work from notebooks and experiments to a deployed research case study. I worked on a content-refresh decision-support problem using a Random Forest model, leakage auditing, validation, and a ranked human-review workflow.

One result also taught me an important lesson about ML evaluation. My model measured about 63.2% accuracy with a random split, but when I tested it using client-grouped validation, the accuracy dropped to about 58.4%.

It would have been easy to only highlight the higher number. Instead, I kept the lower result and changed how I described the system: as directional decision-support rather than something that guarantees correct recommendations.

AI helped me throughout the process with debugging, structuring the portfolio and case study, reviewing explanations, and questioning parts of my approach. But testing the implementation, evaluating the results, and deciding what claims the evidence actually supported remained part of my own workflow.

My next step is to add my Face Recognition Attendance System as a full case study, covering the computer-vision workflow, liveness detection, results, limitations, and lessons from building it.

The biggest thing I’m taking away from this process is that a good portfolio shouldn’t just show successful outputs—it should also show how you built them, what you learned, and where the limitations are.
